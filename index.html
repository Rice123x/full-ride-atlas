Applying for financial aid. Aid is a separate application from admission, with its own forms and its own deadlines — missing them usually means no aid for the whole degree, even if you are admitted. This is the general process; every university differs, so check each one's aid page (linked on its profile).

Step 1 · Work out what kind of money you are chasing

Need-based aidThe university calculates what your family can afford and covers the gap. You apply by submitting financial documents. Common in the US; rare elsewhere.
Merit scholarshipsAwarded for grades, test scores or talent — no financial documents, but often a separate essay and an earlier deadline (frequently ~1 December).
Government scholarshipsRun by a country, not a university. Applied for through an embassy or national portal, usually a year ahead. See the Gov Scholarships tab.
Tuition-free systemsNot aid at all — Germany, Czechia, Iceland and others simply charge no tuition. Your task there is the language requirement and living costs.

Step 2 · Assemble your documents early

Start collecting these months before deadlines — translations and official stamps take weeks in many countries.

  • Parents' income proof — tax returns, employer salary letters, or an official statement of non-filing if your family does not file taxes
  • Bank statements — usually the last 3–12 months for all household accounts
  • Proof of assets — property, business ownership, savings, investments
  • Household details — number of people supported, siblings in education, dependants
  • Special circumstances letter — job loss, illness, currency collapse, single-parent household, supporting relatives. Write this yourself; it is the single most underused document.
  • Certified translations into English, where documents are in another language

Step 3 · Know which form each university wants

CSS ProfileRun by the College Board and used by most US private universities for international need-based aid. Has a fee, but fee waivers exist. This is the form most commonly required.
ISFAAInternational Student Financial Aid Application — a free paper alternative that some universities accept instead of the CSS Profile. Always ask if you cannot afford the CSS fee.
FAFSAUS federal aid. Not for international students — only US citizens and eligible non-citizens. Ignore it unless you hold that status.
University's own formCommon outside the US and at some US schools (Princeton, MIT and Berea all use their own). Check the aid page rather than assuming.

Net Price Calculator · Nearly every US university hosts one. Enter your family's finances and it estimates what you would actually pay. Run it before you apply — it can turn a "too expensive" university into a realistic one, and it is the only way to know whether an ED commitment is affordable.

Step 4 · Apply for aid at the same time as admission

  • Never apply for aid later. At many universities — Stanford among them — international students who do not request aid when applying can never request it in later years.
  • Match the aid deadline to your application round. If you apply Early Decision, the aid forms are usually due in November, not January.
  • Need-aware vs need-blind matters. At need-aware schools, asking for aid is weighed in the admission decision. Ask anyway if you genuinely need it — an unaffordable acceptance is worth nothing.
  • Ask for a fee waiver for the application and CSS Profile. Counsellors can request these, and universities grant them routinely.

Step 5 · Read the award letter properly

An offer that looks generous can still leave a gap. Check each of these before accepting:

  • Grant vs loan — grants are gifts, loans must be repaid. "Aid" totals often blend the two.
  • Is it renewable? For all four years, or only the first? What GPA must you maintain?
  • What is excluded? Flights, visa fees, health insurance, books and personal expenses are often outside the package.
  • Does it cover the summer? Many packages fund only the academic year.
  • Any bond or service condition? Singapore's MOE Tuition Grant carries a three-year work bond; Australia Awards require you to return home for two years.

If the offer is not enough · You may politely appeal. Write to the aid office, state the shortfall in specific numbers, attach evidence of any change in circumstances, and mention competing offers if you have them. Appeals succeed more often than students expect.

ED, EA, REA and RD explained. When you apply to a US university, you choose an application plan. They differ in deadline, whether you are committed if admitted, and whether you can compare financial aid offers before deciding. For international students on a budget, that last point usually matters most.

Early Action (EA) — early answer, no commitment

You submit in November and hear back in December or January, but you are not obliged to attend. You may apply EA to several universities, compare scholarship and aid packages, and decline without penalty. Some universities also run EA II with deadlines around 1–15 January.

Best for · Students whose academic profile is already strong at the start of senior year and who want early results while keeping the freedom to compare offers.

Watch out · Everything must be ready months earlier — fewer chances to improve grades or retake the SAT.

Early Decision (ED) — binding commitment

Applying ED means you promise that if admitted you will enrol and withdraw all other applications. The agreement is signed by you, a parent or guardian, and your school counsellor. Some universities run ED II with a January deadline.

Admission advantage · Many universities admit a noticeably higher share of ED applicants — though the ED pool is also stronger and better prepared, so the gap is not purely a bonus.

The financial aid exception · The one legitimate escape is money. If the aid package genuinely makes attendance unaffordable, you may ask to be released — but only after discussing it with the aid office, and not simply because you changed your mind.

Breaking ED · Doing so without cause is treated as a serious breach: the offer can be rescinded, your counsellor may inform other colleges, and your credibility across the process suffers.

Only apply ED if · The university is your unquestioned first choice, you have researched it thoroughly, and you have already run its Net Price Calculator and believe the result is affordable.

Restrictive Early Action (REA / SCEA) — early, non-binding, restricted

You are not required to attend if admitted, but you agree not to submit certain other early applications while you wait. Policies vary, so read each university's rules — but generally you may not simultaneously apply to another private university's ED, REA or (often) EA programme.

You usually may still apply early to public universities with non-binding EA, to international universities, and to rolling-admission institutions.

Universities commonly using REA/SCEA · Harvard, Yale, Stanford, Princeton and Notre Dame. Policies change — verify before you apply.

Regular Decision (RD) — the standard route

Applications are due in early-to-mid January and decisions arrive in March or early April, with a reply deadline of 1 May. There are no restrictions or commitments.

Best for · Students who need more time to raise grades, retake tests, strengthen activities or refine essays — and anyone who wants to compare every admission and aid offer before choosing.

Watch out · RD is usually the largest and most competitive round by volume.

So which should you choose?

  • Choose EA if you want an early answer while keeping freedom to compare offers. For most international students needing aid, this is the safest early option.
  • Choose ED only if one university is clearly your first choice, you would attend without hesitation, and the Net Price Calculator says you can afford it.
  • Choose REA if your top choice offers it and you accept the restriction on other private early applications.
  • Choose RD if you need more time, or want to weigh every aid package before committing.

For international applicants specifically · Aid policy should drive this decision more than admission-rate statistics. A binding ED commitment removes your ability to compare packages — which is precisely the leverage a student who needs full funding cannot afford to give up.

What the personal essay is actually for

The essay shows you can write clearly and thoughtfully, and it lets an admissions officer hear your voice. The guiding question is simple: what do you want the reader to know about you that grades, courses and test scores cannot show?

Length · 250 words minimum, 650 maximum. As the Common App itself puts it, 650 is your limit, not your goal — use the range if you need it, but do not pad to reach it. Most strong essays land between 500 and 650.

One essay, many universities · The same personal statement goes to every Common App college, so it must not be about any one university. Fit belongs in the supplements.

The seven Common App prompts (2025–26 cycle)

  • 1 · Background & identity — Some students have a background, identity, interest, or talent that is so meaningful they believe their application would be incomplete without it. If this sounds like you, then please share your story.
  • 2 · Setback — The lessons we take from obstacles we encounter can be fundamental to later success. Recount a time when you faced a challenge, setback, or failure. How did it affect you, and what did you learn from the experience?
  • 3 · Questioning a belief — Reflect on a time when you questioned or challenged a belief or idea. What prompted your thinking? What was the outcome?
  • 4 · Gratitude — Reflect on something that someone has done for you that has made you happy or thankful in a surprising way. How has this gratitude affected or motivated you?
  • 5 · Growth — Discuss an accomplishment, event, or realization that sparked a period of personal growth and a new understanding of yourself or others.
  • 6 · Engrossing topic — Describe a topic, idea, or concept you find so engaging that it makes you lose all track of time. Why does it captivate you? What or who do you turn to when you want to learn more?
  • 7 · Free choice — Share an essay on any topic of your choice, including one you have already written or one of your own design.

Practical note · Write your story first, then pick the prompt it fits. Prompt 7 exists precisely so a good story never has to be bent to fit a category.

Six principles that separate strong essays from forgettable ones

  • Small subject, deep treatment. A single afternoon repairing a motorbike with your uncle beats a summary of your whole life. Narrow topics leave room for reflection; broad ones force you into listing.
  • Show the thinking, not just the event. Admissions officers are not scoring your experience — they are reading how your mind works on it. Roughly half your words should be reflection.
  • Be concrete. "I struggled with maths" is invisible. "I failed three consecutive mock papers and started keeping a notebook of every mistake I made" is a person.
  • Sound like yourself. If a sentence is one you would never say aloud, cut it. Thesaurus vocabulary is the most common way students erase their own voice.
  • Change must be visible in behaviour. Claiming growth is weak; showing what you now do differently is strong.
  • The ending should land, not summarise. Avoid "this experience taught me the value of perseverance." Return to a concrete image and let the reader draw the conclusion.

Three structures that reliably work

Moment → meaningOpen inside one specific scene (no scene-setting preamble). Tell what happened. Then spend the second half on what it changed in how you think or act. The most common structure, and the safest.
ThreadPick one recurring object, habit or place — a notebook, a bus route, your grandmother's kitchen — and revisit it at three points in time. The object carries the through-line while you show change around it.
QuestionOpen with a genuine question you could not answer, chase it through two or three attempts, and end somewhere more honest than where you started. Ideal for Prompt 3 and 6.

Rough word budget for a 600-word essay · ~120 words on the opening scene, ~180 on what happened, ~250 on reflection and change, ~50 on the close.

Worked example — the same opening, weak and strong

Both are written here as illustrations, not real students' essays. Compare what each one gives the reader in its first 60 words.

Weak opening

"Ever since I was young, I have always been passionate about engineering. Engineering is a field that combines creativity and problem-solving, and it has the power to change the world. Throughout my life, I have faced many challenges that have taught me the importance of perseverance and hard work, and these qualities have shaped me into the person I am today."

Why it fails: nothing here belongs to one specific person. Every clause could appear in ten thousand other essays. It states qualities instead of demonstrating them, and it has spent 60 words without a single concrete detail.

Strong opening

"The fan my father brought home was already broken, which is why it was cheap. I was eleven, and I took it apart on the kitchen floor to find out why it hummed but would not turn. My mother stepped over the pieces for four days. On the fifth, the blades moved — and I understood that I had not fixed it so much as I had finally understood it."

Why it works: it starts inside a specific moment, gives a detail only this writer would have (a mother stepping over the parts for four days), and ends on a thought that hints at the essay's real subject — the difference between fixing and understanding. It has earned the reader's attention before making any claim about itself.

Common mistakes that sink otherwise good essays

  • The résumé in prose. Listing achievements the rest of the application already covers. The essay is the one place where you are not a list.
  • The manufactured hardship. Writing about a "failure" that is really a humblebrag (an A instead of an A*). Readers see through it instantly.
  • The trauma dump without agency. Real hardship is legitimate material — but the essay must show what you did, not only what happened to you.
  • The saviour narrative. A trip abroad that "opened my eyes" to how much others lack. Almost always centres you at the expense of the people described.
  • The unearned ending. A final paragraph of grand lessons that the story did not actually demonstrate.
  • The mismatched voice. Vocabulary that no seventeen-year-old uses, or a tone borrowed from a sample essay.
  • Answering a different prompt. Especially in supplements: a "why us" essay that never names anything specific about the university.

Revision checklist — run this before you submit

  • Could anyone else have written this essay? If yes, it is not finished.
  • Delete the first paragraph. Is the essay better? Very often it is — most drafts warm up for 80 words before starting.
  • Is at least half of it reflection rather than narration?
  • Does a specific behaviour of yours change by the end?
  • Read it aloud. Every sentence you stumble on needs rewriting.
  • Have you named real things — places, people, objects, numbers — rather than categories?
  • Does the last line resolve an image rather than announce a moral?
  • Has someone who knows you well read it and said "yes, that sounds like you"?
  • Is it between 500 and 650 words, and free of any university's name (unless it is a supplement)?

A realistic timeline

  • June–July — Brainstorm without writing. List 15 small moments that mattered. Do not filter for impressiveness.
  • Late July — Draft two different essays from two different moments. Most students discover their real topic in the second one.
  • August — Rest the drafts a week, then cut ruthlessly. Get one outside reader.
  • September — Finalise the personal statement; start supplements, which multiply fast (MIT, Caltech, Stanford and Harvard each ask for five or more).
  • October — Finish early-round supplements a week before the deadline. Portals get slow on 1 November.
